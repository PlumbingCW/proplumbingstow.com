CIPP Pressure Pipe Lining: A Stow, OH Homeowner Guide

CIPP stands for cured-in-place pipe. A flexible tube soaked in resin is placed inside a damaged pipe and hardened, which creates a new pipe within the old one without digging a long trench. In Stow, OH, this method is common for sewer and drain lines. Lining a pressurized water supply line is a more specialized process, and it has limited availability for houses.

The phrase CIPP pressure pipe lining mixes two different jobs, so this guide separates them. A plumber should confirm which method, if any, fits the pipe in your home, because pipe material, diameter, bends, and damage all change the answer.

The proven use

How CIPP lining works in a drain or sewer pipe

A crew first cleans the old pipe, often with a jetting nozzle, and inspects it with a camera. A felt tube saturated with epoxy or similar resin is then pulled or inverted into the line. The tube is inflated against the pipe wall and cured with hot water, steam, or ultraviolet light.

Once hard, the resin forms a smooth, jointless liner. Roots that entered through joints have nowhere to grip, and small cracks are sealed from inside. A liner adds thickness and strength, but it also reduces the inside diameter by a small amount, which matters on narrow lines.

Why water supply lines are a different story

A sewer or drain runs mostly by gravity and holds little pressure. A water supply line is under constant pressure and carries drinking water, so every material that touches that water has to be suitable for it. Residential supply lines also have many tight bends, fittings, and branch connections to the kitchen, baths, and laundry.

Some firms apply an epoxy coating inside small pipes, and structural liners exist for larger pressurized mains. These are specialized processes with limited residential use, and not every plumber offers them. For many houses with failing supply pipe, repiping with new copper or PEX remains the more common path.

What a liner cannot do

A liner does not fix a pipe that has sagged and holds water, and it does not change the slope. It also cannot repair a bad connection where the lateral meets the county main. Those spots may need a dig or a separate repair.

Lining also does not replace maintenance. Grease, wipes, and flushed debris still clog a lined pipe, so the habits that keep drains clear still apply after the work is done.

Checklist before you agree to lining

  • 1Get a camera inspection first, so the plan is based on the actual pipe and not a guess.
  • 2Ask what material the pipe is and how the liner bonds to it, whether clay, cast iron, or plastic.
  • 3Ask whether the line has collapsed or badly offset sections, since lining may not fix those.
  • 4Confirm that any liner touching drinking water is rated for that use.
  • 5Ask how the connections to the fixtures and the main will be reopened and tested.
  • 6Ask for a written, clear price before work starts, and compare it with dig-and-replace.

Common mistakes with pipe lining

Lining without a camera look

A liner cannot fix what the crew has not seen. Belly sections that hold water or crushed pipe may need excavation instead.

Treating every pipe the same

A method that suits a clay sewer lateral does not automatically suit a copper supply line under pressure.

Skipping the cleaning step

Roots and scale left in the pipe leave a rough, weak liner. Good prep is half the result.

Ignoring the cost of reduced diameter

On a 4 inch line the loss is small. On a smaller line, check that flow capacity still meets your needs.

When to call a plumber

Call when a drain backs up more than once, when roots show on a camera, or when a sewer line has cracks but is still mostly intact. For lines that have collapsed, sewer line repair by excavation or a pipe bursting method may fit better than lining.

Lining compared with replacement

Lining tends to fit when

  • The pipe is cracked or root-invaded but still round
  • You want to avoid digging up a driveway or landscaping
  • The line has few severe bends
  • A camera shows the host pipe is sound enough to bond to

Replacement tends to fit when

  • The pipe has collapsed or has a belly that holds water
  • The material is failing along its whole length
  • It is a pressurized supply line with many fittings
  • The new route can be opened up without major damage
