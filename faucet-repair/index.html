Why faucets wear out faster around Stow

Moderately hard water

The city's 2024 water quality report gives hardness of about 7 grains per gallon on average. That scale clogs aerators, grips cartridges and roughens the seats that drips start from, so a faucet that runs rough is often just chalked up inside.

Freeze and thaw cycles

Northeast Ohio winters cycle between freezing and thawing. Hose bibs and exterior walls take the abuse, and a split that happens in January shows itself in April.

Homes from the 1960s to 1990s

Older Census data puts the median Stow home at about 1980. Original shutoff valves in homes from that era often have stiff stems that fail when someone finally turns them.

Hydrant flushing

The city says hydrant flushing can briefly change water pressure. Sputtering or cloudy water after a flush is not always a faucet fault, so run the tap for a minute before calling.

A drip that keeps time

One drop every few seconds from the spout means a worn washer, seat or cartridge seal.

Water at the base of the handle

Failed O-rings or a loose packing nut push water up around the stem.

Low flow from one spout

Mineral buildup in the aerator or a clogged cartridge port is the usual cause.

A wet cabinet floor

A weeping supply line, nut or valve under the sink wets the base long before you see it above.

Hot and cold mixing wrong

A worn cartridge or ceramic disc can no longer balance the two sides.

A handle that spins freely

The stem or set screw has stripped, and the faucet needs an internal part.

Emergency

Urgent: a faucet line is spraying or a valve will not close

Some faucet failures cannot wait for morning. A burst braided supply line under a vanity can push a steady stream, and a shower valve or tub spout that will not turn off keeps running into the drain or onto the floor. If the shutoff valve under the sink is frozen in place, the problem becomes a whole-house shutoff question.

1

Shut it under the fixture

Turn the oval handle on each supply valve clockwise until snug. Do not use pliers on a stiff stem.

2

Go to the main if the stop fails

The main shutoff is usually near the water meter. Close it and open one low faucet to drain the lines.

3

Protect the floor

Set towels at the cabinet base, empty the cabinet and set a bucket under the leaking line.

4

Keep power away from water

If a leak reaches an outlet, switch the breaker off from a dry spot and stay clear.

Stow's water meter is read by radio, and the bill shows a read code. A spraying line that ran all night will show up on the next real reading, so tell the dispatcher how long the water ran. A leak on the house side of the meter is the homeowner's, and the water department can say where its own responsibility ends.

Compression faucets are the old two-handle style, and they drip because the rubber washer wears against the seat. Cartridge and ball designs often show up in single-handle kitchen and bath faucets, and ceramic discs are common on newer fixtures. Naming the type matters because the wrong replacement part will not seat. A plumber pulls the handle, reads the brand and stem, and matches the repair kit.

Under-sink valves and angle stops that burst or stick

The small valve under a sink, called an angle stop, is the only thing between you and a whole-house shutoff. When it leaks at the stem, a packing nut tweak can fix it. When it is stuck or cracked, the plumber replaces it, which usually means shutting the main for a short time. The same goes for toilet valves, which are discussed on the toilet repair page.

Shower valves, showerheads and bathtub faucets

A showerhead that drips after the water is off is usually a worn cartridge or a buildup of scale in the valve, and a drip behind the wall is the harder case. Showerheads clog with deposits in the nozzles, and soaking the head in vinegar often brings the spray back. A tub spout that dribbles or a diverter that sends water to the head and spout together points to a failing diverter or cartridge.

When the leak is inside the wall, stained drywall or a wet ceiling below may be the first clue. At that point the work may involve leak detection before any wall is opened. Slow shower or tub drains are a different problem. A hair clog in the strainer or trap is the usual cause, and drain cleaning covers the tools that clear it.

Laundry sinks, utility sinks and outdoor spigots

Utility and laundry sinks in basements often leak at the faucet base, the trap or the washer hoses. Check the washer hoses, since a cracked rubber one floods a laundry room fast. An outdoor hose bib that is left connected through winter can freeze, split the pipe behind it and leak inside the wall when the thaw comes. Disconnect hoses before the first hard freeze.

Repair or replace the faucet

Repair makes sense when

  • The body is sound and only a seal or cartridge is worn
  • The finish is good and parts are easy to find
  • The fixture is a newer model from a known brand

Replace when

  • The body is pitted, cracked or corroded through
  • The finish is peeling, and parts are discontinued
  • Repairs keep coming back within a season

Repair is cheaper today, and replacement is cheaper over a decade if the faucet is old and failing repeatedly. A new faucet is also an easy time to add a new supply line and angle stop. Remodels that move or add fixtures are a bigger job, covered under plumbing installation.
