The first decision

Fix the old toilet or put in a new one

Most toilet trouble comes down to a few cheap parts inside the tank. A flapper, a fill valve or a wax ring can be swapped in one visit, and the porcelain keeps working for years afterward. A new toilet makes sense when the bowl or tank is cracked, the glaze is crazed, or the model keeps clogging no matter what is done to the drain.

Repair makes sense when

  • The porcelain is sound and the flush only fails sometimes
  • A flapper, fill valve or flush lever is worn
  • Water seeps at the base and the floor is still firm
  • The supply line or shutoff valve is the only leaking part

Replace when

  • The tank or bowl has a hairline or open crack
  • The floor around the base is soft or stained
  • The toilet clogs weekly even with a clear drain
  • Parts for an older model are hard to find

Emergency

Toilet overflowing or clogged right now: an urgent job

A toilet that is rising toward the rim is an urgent problem, especially upstairs where the ceiling below takes the damage. Do not flush again hoping it will clear. Each flush sends more water into a bowl that has nowhere to go.

Do this in the next two minutes

Reach behind the toilet and turn the small oval or round handle on the supply valve clockwise until it stops. Lift the tank lid and push the flapper down by hand if the tank is still refilling. If water has reached an outlet or power strip, stay clear and shut the breaker off from a dry spot.

For a bowl that is full but not rising, a flange plunger pressed in a firm seal over the drain hole is the right first tool. Use slow pushes and one sharp pull. A closet auger, a short cable with a hook, reaches the trap without scratching the porcelain when a plunger will not move the blockage.

If two toilets or a tub also back up, the clog is deeper, and drain cleaning equipment is the right answer rather than another plunge.

Running, phantom flushing and slow refills

A running toilet wastes water around the clock, and the trickle you hear from the tank adds up on the Stow water bill. Open the tank and look. If the flapper does not drop back flat over the opening, or its rubber feels slimy or warped, it leaks into the bowl. If the water level creeps up to the overflow tube, the fill valve is not shutting off.

Stow's water averages about 7 grains per gallon according to the city's 2024 water quality report, which is moderately hard. That scale builds on fill valve seats and flapper edges, so a part that looked fine two seasons ago can start to hiss. A new fill valve and flapper kit is usually a short visit, and a stiff flush lever or a chain that is too long gets sorted at the same time.

Leaks at the base: wax ring, flange and closet bolts

A toilet sits on a wax or foam ring that seals the bowl outlet to the floor flange. When that ring fails, water escapes at the base on every flush, rots the subfloor and can drip into a ceiling or crawl space below. The fix means shutting off the supply, draining the bowl, lifting the toilet and setting a new ring.

The flange is the metal or plastic ring bolted to the floor. If it is cracked, rusted or sitting below the finished floor after a tile job, a new ring cannot seal properly. A flange repair ring or spacer fixes that, and the closet bolts that hold the toilet down get replaced with fresh ones. Soft flooring, a musty smell or discoloration around the base are signs that the leak has been going on for a while.

Supply lines, shutoff valves and rocking toilets

The braided line from the wall valve to the tank gets stiff with age, and the valve itself can seize or weep from the stem. Never force a valve that will not turn. A burst or stuck angle stop is covered on the faucet and valve repair page, and a plumber can replace it with a quarter-turn style that works smoothly.

A toilet that rocks is loose at the floor. Tightening the nuts may be enough, but if the flange sits low or the floor is uneven, shims or a flange fix are needed. Cranking the bolts down on a wobbly bowl is how people crack porcelain, so do not overtighten.

Installing a new toilet: removal, flange and fit

A replacement starts with measuring the rough-in, which is the distance from the finished wall to the center of the floor drain. In general terms, many homes use a standard rough-in, but older bathrooms vary, and a toilet that does not match leaves a gap or a bowl pressed against the wall. A plumber checks this before the old fixture comes out.

Old toilet removal means draining the tank, disconnecting the supply, lifting the bowl and scraping off the old ring. Then the flange is inspected, a new ring and bolts go in, and the toilet is set level. A one-piece model wipes clean easily but is heavy. A bidet seat needs a nearby outlet and a tee on the supply line, and the plumber can fit one while the new toilet goes in.

Water-saving models flush with less water per cycle, and a good one clears the bowl in one flush. Ask which flush system is used, since pressure-assist and gravity tanks sound and behave differently. Bathroom remodels that move the toilet are a bigger job, covered under plumbing installation.

Flushables and the county sewer

Summit County's sewer backup page says backups caused by private lateral obstructions or non-flushable items are not a justifiable claim. Wipes, paper towels and feminine products are the usual culprits, so keep them out of the bowl and out of the lateral you will pay to clear.
