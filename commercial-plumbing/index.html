A commercial plumbing emergency after hours

Business plumbing emergencies rarely wait for a convenient hour. A broken supply line behind a restaurant wall, a floor drain pushing water across a shop floor, or a toilet overflowing in a shared restroom can ruin stock, flooring and drywall before the next morning. The first few minutes matter more than anything a plumber does later.

Everyone who opens or closes your building should know where the main shutoff is and how it turns. Many sit near the meter, in a utility room or in a mechanical closet. A valve that has not moved in years may be stiff, so it is worth finding it before the night it counts.

Water near electrical equipment

If water is reaching a breaker panel, a wall outlet, a walk-in cooler compressor or a floor-mounted machine, stay away and shut off power only if you can reach the switch while standing on dry ground. Otherwise leave it and tell the dispatcher when you call. If you smell gas, leave the building, then call 911 or the gas utility from outside before you phone a plumber.

1

Close the main shutoff

Turn the main water valve clockwise until it stops. If the leak is on one fixture, its own stop valve under the sink or behind the toilet may be enough.

2

Stop feeding the drain

Tell staff to stop using sinks, dishwashers and toilets that share the backed-up line. More water down a blocked drain only adds to the overflow.

3

Protect what you can

Lift boxes, paper goods and electronics off the floor. Mops and a wet vacuum help, but do not wade into standing water near live equipment.

4

Know your building

Have the street address, the unit or suite, and any access or alarm details ready. If the building has a property manager, note who holds the keys.

Floor drains, lift pumps and restroom overflows

Floor drains do quiet work until they stop. In a shop or back-of-house kitchen they carry mop water, equipment drips and washdown. When one overflows, the cause is often a clogged trap, a blocked branch line, or a lift or ejector pump that has stopped moving water up to the sewer. Callers describing these jobs often mention a drain grate that must come off right now, or standing water that will not leave.

A lift or ejector pump sits in a sealed pit below the slab or basement floor and pushes waste uphill. If its float sticks or the motor burns out, the pit fills and water shows up at the lowest fixture. A pump alarm, a gurgle from a floor drain, or a restroom that drains slowly after a flush are the usual early warnings.

Restroom overflow

A toilet that rises and will not settle usually points to a blockage in the branch or the main. Flushing again makes it worse.

Floor drain gurgling

Air bubbling up through a drain while another fixture runs means the line is partly blocked or poorly vented.

Pump alarm or silence

A lift pump that hums without moving water, or one that never runs while the pit is full, has a failed impeller, float or motor.

Standing water that stays

Water that sits in a floor drain for hours points to a deeper blockage than a surface clog.

Sewage smell indoors

A dry trap, a cracked pipe or a failed seal can let sewer air in. Treat a strong smell as a reason to call, not to air out and wait.

Water in the wrong room

A wet ceiling tile, a damp wall base or a soft spot in the floor can mean a supply or drain leak hidden behind finishes.

Commercial drain and grease problems

Kitchens, bakeries and food prep spaces send fat and food solids down their drains, and those solids harden in the pipe. A grill line or three-compartment sink that drains slower each week is telling you something. Our commercial drain cleaning work uses cable machines to clear the blockage, and heavier buildup may call for a different method once a plumber has seen the line.

We do get calls asking about grease trap cleaning and pumping. Ask the dispatcher when you call so we can tell you plainly whether the work fits what we send out or needs a hauler who pumps traps. A trap that is overdue and full will push grease downstream, which is how a small maintenance task becomes a restaurant-wide backup.

Backflow testing for Stow businesses

A backflow preventer is a valve assembly that keeps used or contaminated water from flowing back into the drinking supply. Businesses most often have one on a fire line, an irrigation system, a boiler feed or a commercial kitchen connection. Stow runs a backflow prevention program: devices must be tested annually, and the system has over 1,500 approved devices.

If your building has a device, plan on a test every year and keep the paperwork where the next manager can find it. Callers often tell us a notice arrived and they are not sure when the last test happened. Ask the City of Stow how your device is recorded, and tell the dispatcher what kind of device it is and where it sits so the right visit can be arranged.

Leak detection in commercial buildings

Commercial leaks are harder to chase than a leak under one sink. Pipes run through ceilings, walls, slabs and shared chases, and a water bill that keeps climbing may trace to a line nobody can see. Our leak detection page explains the tools a plumber uses, such as acoustic listening gear and thermal imaging.

Here is the honest part. Commercial jobs usually need an on-site look before anyone can say what the fix involves. A plumber cannot tell you over the phone whether you have a pinhole in copper, a failed fitting or a buried line, so treat any early talk as a starting point.

Water heaters and restroom fixtures

A business water heater works hard, and Stow water arrives at about 7 grains per gallon on average, according to the city's 2024 water quality report. That is moderately hard water, which leaves scale on heating elements and the tank bottom. A heater that rumbles, runs out early or leaks at the base needs a look, and our water heater repair page covers what that visit involves.

Restroom fixtures cause plenty of calls too: running toilets, loose flush valves, leaking faucets and failing supply stops. Fixing a small fixture problem early is far cheaper than cleaning up after one that was left to run all weekend.

Property managers and multi-unit buildings

Small multi-unit buildings add a layer of complication. A tenant calls about a backup, but the blockage may sit in a shared line serving three units, or the leak may start in the unit above. Tell the dispatcher which unit is affected, whether neighbors see the same symptom, and who can give access after hours.

Repeated calls for the same drain are a pattern worth reporting. Picture a four-unit building where the first-floor kitchen backs up every few months: that is usually a shared line problem, not a tenant habit, and it deserves a camera look or a deeper clearing rather than another quick fix. We also cover nearby towns, including Hudson, when your portfolio spans more than one.

A repair makes sense when

  • The problem is one fixture, valve or short pipe run
  • The pipe is otherwise sound and accessible
  • The building is leased and the fix must be quick
  • A part, such as a float or flush valve, has failed

Larger work makes sense when

  • The same line backs up again and again
  • A pump pit has failed repeatedly
  • Pipe is corroded or leaking in several places
  • A shared stack serves many units

Licensing: what Ohio requires and how to check

The state of Ohio licenses commercial plumbing contractors through the Ohio Construction Industry Licensing Board. A business can confirm a contractor's license on the Ohio eLicense lookup before hiring. We do not publish a license number on this page, so ask the dispatcher any questions about who will do the work and they can answer before anyone is sent.

Residential plumbing contractors are handled differently, and the local building department decides registration. For commercial work, the state license is the check that counts. For permits, ask the City of Stow Building Department before work starts.
