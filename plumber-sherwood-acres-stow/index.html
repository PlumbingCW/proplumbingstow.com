Storm water rules

Why sump and downspout water must stay out of the sewer

Stow Codified Ordinance 925.01 prohibits connecting roof drains, downspouts and other storm water conveyances from any building or land to the sanitary sewer. In plain terms, sump pump discharge and downspout water belong in the yard or the storm drain and never in the sanitary line.

That matters because the sanitary line goes to the county system. Summit County's sewer backup page says backups caused by extreme weather, private lateral obstructions or non-flushable items are not a justifiable claim under Ordinance 119A. A plumber can tell whether a blockage sits in your private lateral.

Flood history

What Stow's flood history means for a basement

The City of Stow's own timeline notes the 1913 Ohio flood and record flooding in Stow and Munroe Falls during the storms of July 2003. Those are city-wide events, and nothing on this page says Sherwood Acres was hit by either. The lesson is general: a working sump pump, a battery backup and a clear discharge path matter in a city with that history.

If your basement has taken on water before, a sewer line inspection helps rule out the lateral as a contributing cause.

Housing age

Housing age and what it means for pipes

Older Census estimates put the median year a Stow home was built at 1980 across the whole city. That is not a Sherwood Acres figure. Check your county auditor record for your own year, and have a plumber look at your actual pipes before replacing anything.

Permits and septic

Permits, and how to tell sewer from septic

Sanitary sewer permits are not handled by the Stow Building Department. They go to the Summit County Department of Sanitary Services at 330-926-2409. A home that gets a quarterly county sewer bill is usually on public sewer. Summit Public Health says about 33,000 homes in the county have septic systems, so confirm which you have before ordering any sewer work.

Plumbing permits for the house itself come through Stow Building and Engineering at 330-689-2729.
