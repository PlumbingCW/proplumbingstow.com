Repiping in Stow, OH

Repiping in Stow, OH means replacing the water supply pipes inside a house, either all of them or one worn section, with new copper or PEX. It is a big job, so the first question should be whether you need it at all. One leak in a good pipe is a repair. Rusty water, low pressure at every tap and pinholes that keep returning in different spots are the pattern that tilts the answer toward new pipe.

Repair or repipe: a plain decision guide

A pipe that leaks once is not always a pipe that is finished. A plumber weighs three things: how many leaks have happened, what material the pipe is, and how it looks on the inside where a cut section can be examined. The honest rule of thumb is that a repair is cheaper today and replacement is cheaper over the next several years if failures keep coming.

Use the two columns below as a first pass. If your house lands mostly on the right, schedule a visit and have the plumber open a few access points and check the pipe wall.

Repair is the sensible call when

  • There has been one leak, at a fitting or valve
  • The pipe near the leak looks clean and thick-walled
  • Pressure and water color are normal at every fixture
  • You plan to sell soon and the pipe is serviceable

Repipe when

  • Pinholes appear in several places over time
  • Water runs brown or orange after sitting
  • Pressure is weak throughout the house
  • The pipe is galvanized steel or polybutylene

Signs your pipes are near the end

Brown or orange water at the tap

Rust from galvanized lines shows up first thing in the morning or after the house has been away.

Pressure that is weak everywhere

Mineral and rust buildup narrows the pipe, and a single fixture repair will not cure it.

Pinhole leaks in different rooms

A repair on the second floor followed by another in the basement says the whole system is aging together.

Repeated patches on the same run

Each patch shifts the stress to the next weak point, so the repair bills add up.

Older Census data puts the median Stow home at 1980, with about 32% built from 1960 to 1979 and 38% from 1980 to 1999. Homes from this era often have early copper, and some older ones still have galvanized lines.

The polybutylene window

Late 1970s through mid 1990s houses may contain polybutylene, a gray or blue plastic pipe that was widely used and later phased out. Look at the pipe near the water heater or where the main comes in. If it matches, have a plumber assess it before it leaks.

Planning a repipe: what to decide before booking

A whole-house repipe takes planning because water is off for part of each day while pipes are swapped. Think about when the house is quietest, whether anyone needs water for medical or work reasons, and where a temporary supply can be set up.

Bring a few items to the first visit: your water bills, a list of past leaks and where they were, and any photos of the pipe near the heater or the main. The more history the plumber sees, the better the recommendation.

Whole house or partial

A partial repipe replaces a bad stretch, such as the lines to a bathroom group or the run in an addition. It costs less than a full job and can be a good fit when only one zone is troubled. A whole-house job makes sense when failure is spreading, when the material is a known problem type, or when you plan to stay for many years.

PEX or copper

PEX is flexible, goes in with fewer joints and tolerates freezing better, so it is often the faster and less invasive choice. Copper has a long track record, handles heat well and suits exposed runs, but it needs more fittings and more labor. Neither is right for every house, and a good plumber will explain the trade-off for yours.

What the work involves

1

Inspect and map

The plumber traces the supply lines, checks the material, and plans where new pipe will run.

2

Open small access points

Instead of tearing out walls, the plumber cuts targeted openings in drywall, usually in closets, behind fixtures and in basement ceilings.

3

Install and connect

New lines are run to each fixture, then tied into the main and the water heater, with water shut off for part of the day.

4

Pressure test and close up

The system is tested under pressure, inspected where required, and the openings are left ready for patching.

Drywall repair and paint are usually a separate trade, so plan for it. Work in a finished basement ceiling or a tiled bathroom costs more time than an open crawlspace run. If a service line also needs attention, see water service line repair to understand that part of the system.
