Sewer Camera Inspection in Stow, OH

A sewer camera inspection in Stow, OH sends a small waterproof camera on a flexible cable through your home's drain line so a plumber can watch the inside of the pipe on a screen. It shows cracks, roots, sags, offset joints, buildup and anything that should not be there, without digging. Same-day appointments are available when the schedule allows.

Most people book one for a reason: a home purchase, backups that keep returning, or a plan to reline or replace a pipe. The camera answers what the line looks like today, and the plumber explains what that means before you decide on any repair.

When a camera inspection is worth booking

The best time for an inspection is before a problem forces your hand. Buying a house is the classic case, since the lateral under the yard is out of sight and the seller may not know its condition. Repeat backups, slow drains in several fixtures at once and gurgling toilets are the other common triggers.

A camera is also the right step before lining or replacing a pipe, because the plumber needs to see the length, the bends and the damage to choose a method. For a basic clog, sewer line cleaning often comes first, and the camera follows if the blockage returns.

Backups that keep coming back

A line cleared and blocked again within weeks usually has a cause the camera can show, such as roots or a belly.

Several fixtures drain slowly

When the tub, floor drain and sink all slow down together, the main line is a better suspect than any single trap.

Sewer smell indoors or in the yard

Persistent odor can mean a cracked pipe, a dry trap or a failed connection. The camera helps tell which.

Damp or sunken ground over the line

A soft patch of yard, a new dip or unusually lush grass can mark a break that lets sewage escape.

Buying or selling a house

An inspection with recorded video gives both sides a shared record of the line's condition.

Planning a repair or lining

Before any trenchless or open-cut work, the plumber needs a clear picture of the pipe.

What the camera actually shows

The camera head carries lights and a lens, and it is pushed through a cleanout, a pulled toilet or a roof vent opening. On the screen, the plumber watches the pipe interior as it moves along, calling out defects and noting the distance. Many systems record the video and save stills for your records.

Pipe material is easy to tell on screen. Clay tile shows joints at regular spacing, cast iron looks rough and scaled, and PVC is smooth and pale. Each fails differently, so what you see guides the next step.

Locating the line and marking the problem

A camera that carries a transmitter can be traced from above with a locator. The plumber walks the surface, reads the signal and notes the depth, so you know exactly where the pipe runs and where the problem sits. That matters because homeowners often guess the path wrong, and a dig started a few feet off misses the break.

What a good report should include

Ask for the recorded video, the distance to each defect, the pipe material, the depth of the line, and a plain statement of what was and was not reached. A report that says the camera stopped at a collapse is honest and more useful than a vague all-clear.

What this means for a Stow property

Sewer service is through the county

The City of Stow does not provide or maintain sanitary sewer service. Contact the Summit County Department of Environmental Services at 330-926-2400. The county bills quarterly based on the city's meter reads, so a quarterly county sewer bill usually means you are on public sewer rather than septic.

Who owns what

The county page does not say who owns the lateral, so ask Summit County Environmental Services before assuming. A camera tells you what is wrong, and the county can tell you who handles it.

Backup claims

The county's sewer backup page says backups caused by extreme weather, private lateral obstructions or non-flushable items are not a justifiable claim (Ordinance 119A). A recorded inspection that shows whether the blockage is in your lateral or the main gives you a record when you speak with the county.

Downspouts and the sanitary line

Stow Codified Ordinance 925.01 prohibits connecting roof drains, downspouts or other storm water conveyances to the sanitary sewer. A camera, along with smoke or dye testing, can reveal whether storm water has been tied into the line.

A word on older homes

Older Census data puts the median Stow home at 1980, with about 18% built before 1960. Homes from the 1960s and 70s often have cast iron or clay drains that crack or shift with age. That does not mean yours is failing. It means a camera is a useful first look when something seems off.

What happens after the camera

Cleaning is enough when

  • The pipe wall is intact
  • The buildup is grease, scale or soft debris
  • Roots are light and joints are tight
  • No sag is holding water

Plan a repair when

  • The pipe is cracked, collapsed or offset
  • Roots keep returning through open joints
  • A belly holds standing water
  • A downspout or storm line connects to the sewer

If the camera finds damage, the next step is described on the sewer line repair page. Sanitary sewer permits are not handled by the Stow Building Department, and applications go to the Summit County Department of Sanitary Services at 330-926-2409. If water seems to be escaping from a supply pipe rather than a drain, a leak detection visit is the better tool.
